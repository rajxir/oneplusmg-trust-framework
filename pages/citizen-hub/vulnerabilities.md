## Genomics and vulnerability

[Genomics](glossary#genomics) offers unprecedented opportunities to improve our health. Understanding how our [genes](glossary#gene) and their small [variations](glossary#variant) influence our risks of developing diseases and our response to drugs improves diagnosis, treatment and prevention, and enables more [personalised medicine](glossary#personalised-medicine). At the same time, genomics may also raise vulnerabilities that we must carefully consider. On the one hand, genetic test results can reveal inherent susceptibilities to disease, called genetic predispositions. While this information can help take targeted actions, such as adopting a preventive lifestyle, it may sometimes cause more harm than good, for instance when no effective or affordable treatment or preventive measures are available. On the other hand, genomic information can be misused, for example, leading to stigma or discrimination, to which rare disease patients are particularly vulnerable. For these reasons, it is essential to establish safeguards and trustworthy frameworks to prevent data misuse and help individuals manage their genetic predispositions.

## Genetic predisposition to disease

Genetic testing, where individuals have some parts of their [genome](glossary#genome) sequenced and analysed, is becoming increasingly common. People may seek testing due to a family history of an inherited disease (see the topic [When a family member falls ill](cascade-screening)), for reproductive planning (see the topic [Before pregnancy](preconception-carrier-screening)), or out of curiosity through [direct-to-consumer tests](glossary#direct-to-consumer-test) (see the topic [Heritage](heritage)). Hence, genetic results can vary widely in scope and severity, but they often highlight an important reality: everyone has some genetic predispositions to illness.

While we all know that illness is possible, and probably an unavoidable part of life, this awareness becomes much more concrete when a genetic test suggests an increased risk of conditions such as cancer, diabetes or Alzheimer’s disease. In this way, genomics can confront people with their inherent vulnerability as human beings. For deeper insight into how people experience and make sense of vulnerabilities in genomics, [see this academic study on public perspectives](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-01580-7).

Becoming aware of one’s inherent vulnerabilities can have a significant emotional and social impact. It may cause fear or anxiety, affect family relationships or raise difficult questions—also because genomic information often involves uncertainty and probabilities rather than clear answers. Interpreting these health risks can be challenging—see the topic page [Interpreting results](uncertainties) for more information.

Although disease risks cannot be entirely eliminated, people can learn to manage their genetic predispositions and the feelings this information may provoke. Professional counselling plays a crucial role here in supporting understanding and targeted decision-making. Moreover, helping people be more familiar with genomics can reinforce the awareness that, despite genetic predispositions, individuals are more than their [DNA](glossary#dna) and retain autonomy over their lives.

## Rare diseases and their specific challenges

[Since most rare diseases have a genetic origin](https://erdera.org/article/genetic-and-molecular-foundations-understanding-the-dna-roots-of-rare-diseases/), their understanding has rapidly improved thanks to genomic [sequencing](glossary#sequencing) and related research and data-sharing activities. Achieving an accurate diagnosis can end a long [diagnostic odyssey](https://www.genomicseducation.hee.nhs.uk/genotes/knowledge-hub/the-diagnostic-odyssey-in-rare-disease/) and may enable earlier intervention, treatment options or informed reproductive planning. However, identifying a [rare disease or genetic predisposition](https://www.eurordis.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/RB_NBS_report_vff.pdf) can also introduce other vulnerabilities, particularly regarding the affordability or accessibility of treatments or regarding privacy.

### Unaffordable or inaccessible therapies

Where treatments do exist, they may be extremely expensive and offer uncertain benefits. Some rare disease therapies receive accelerated approval to allow faster patient access, which can be crucial for rapidly progressing conditions, such as spinal muscular atrophy (SMA). Nonetheless, accelerated approval to therapies raises legitimate concerns about these therapies’ long-term effectiveness and safety. As a result, some high-cost treatments may place families and healthcare systems under enormous pressure while offering uncertain outcomes.

> **Real story – Zolgensma: ‘the most expensive drug ever’**
>
> In 2019, the pharmaceutical laboratory Novartis launched Zolgensma at a price of €1.9 million, making it the [most expensive drug at the time](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/medicine-access/novartis-switzerland-worlds-most-expensive-drug/75388576#:~:text=Zolgensma%2C%20a%20one%2Doff%20treatment,markets%20prove%20tough%20to%20crack). Zolgensma is a one-time treatment for infants with spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), a rare genetic disorder that leads to progressive muscle weakness and often death in early childhood.
>
> Zolgensma has greatly improved outcomes for SMA. If given pre-symptomatically, some doctors believe it can approach a cure. Earlier treatment generally yields better results and this promise puts families in a [desperate race against time](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2022/mar/05/real-life-hunger-games-lifesaving-drug-costs-2m-dollars). Yet, clinicians also warn it is not a miracle drug: the average age of diagnosis is five months, which often comes too late to reverse established damage.
>
> Novartis has been criticised for selling the drug at such a high price, prioritising commercial interest above human wellbeing. However, Novartis argues that its pricing reflects years of research and development, the limited patient population and the potential of averting lifetime treatment costs. Nevertheless, many countries are not able to fund the drug. Governments try to negotiate steep discounts with pharmaceutical companies, but the details of these negotiations often remain opaque. Approval and reimbursement criteria also vary by country, and many families end up turning to crowdfunding to cover the costs when they can’t get coverage.
>
> A widely cited case is that of [*baby Pia* in Belgium](https://www.brusselstimes.com/72812/belgian-toddler-pia-has-received-treatment-with-life-saving-drug). Pia was born with the most severe form of SMA in 2019, when Zolgensma was not yet approved or reimbursed in Belgium. The parents asked Novartis for compassionate access or a donation, but it refused. The family then crowdfunded the full amount in just a few days, sparking national controversy and public debate on the need for structural solutions.

### Privacy

Rare diseases affect small populations, which means genomic data linked to them are inherently scarce. When datasets include related data of family members and unique genetic variants, [identifying individuals becomes even easier](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35154238/). We can use the analogy of a small village: describing a person with a distinctive trait and family connections makes it easy to identify them. The same applies to rare disease data.

These risks are heightened by international data sharing, essential for rare disease research. Indeed, single-country datasets are often too small to produce robust scientific evidence, making cross-border collaboration necessary. This increases the importance of strong privacy protections and responsible data governance. For more information, see the page on [Privacy](privacy).

### Empowering rare disease patients

In the context of rare diseases , genetic information is—above all—a source of empowerment. To support ongoing breakthroughs in diagnosis and treatment, continued investments in genomic research and its integration into clinical practice are essential. However, scientific and clinical progress and decisions on how to manage rare diseases do not only depend on scientific, technical, or commercial arguments. Progress in rare diseases [should not only be guided by what is technically or commercially viable](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9160156/). When deciding how to allocate resources or when negotiating the price of a test or therapy, societal and ethical considerations are equally important. Greater transparency and public debate are crucial to move beyond the current opacity, as well as involving patient organisations.This can shape research priorities, care pathways and reimbursement policies, and help patients access registries, specialist centres and clinical trials, which are often the only route to treatment.

> **Real story – From unknown mutation to a custom-made drug**
>
> A six-years old girl, named Mila Makovec, had a rare and fatal genetic disorder: the Batten disease. She was progressively losing her ability to see, speak, and walk. Doctors had already carried out genetic testing, but they could only find one of the two mutations causing her disease. Everything changed when a research team at [Boston Children's Hospital](https://answers.childrenshospital.org/milasen-batten-disease/) decided to sequence her entire DNA, including regions that are normally ignored. After carefully analysing the data, they discovered a hidden mutation: a piece of extra DNA inserted into one of her genes, disrupting how it worked.
>
> This discovery did more than giving a diagnosis—it opened a new possibility. The researchers designed a completely new, personalised therapy aimed at fixing the specific error in Mila’s gene. The treatment, later named milasen, was developed in less than a year—something never done before for a single patient—thanks to unprecedented collaboration between researchers, the drug industry and its regulatory system, and a fund-raising foundation. When Mila began receiving the treatment, her condition began to stabilise. Her seizures became shorter and less frequent, and she showed some improvements in functions like swallowing and movement. While the treatment was not curative and Mila Mila ultimately succumbed to her illness, the therapy changed the course of her disease and gave her and her family more time together, as well as hope for a potential cure in the future for other children.

## Discrimination and stigmatisation

Genomic data are powerful for society as a whole to tackle both rare and common conditions. They offer significant value to individuals, their families and future generations. However, whether genomic data have positive or negative implications largely depends on how they are actually used . If genomic data are misused, [individuals may encounter discrimination or stigmatisation](https://zenodo.org/records/14720403) based on genetic characteristics they cannot control. Therefore, people may fear that employers, insurers, banks or other organisations could leverage genetic information—such as disease predispositions—to deny employment, raise insurance premiums or limit access to financial or social services.

> **Real story – The Holmes case**
>
> In 2024, 17-year-old Carys Holmes was refused entry to the British Army because of a family history of breast cancer. Two relatives, who had breast cancer, carried a [mutation](glossary#mutation) in the BRCA1 gene, which significantly increases the risk of developing breast and ovarian cancer. This family history means that Carys had a 50% chance of carrying the genetic mutation, even though she had not been tested and did not have any current illness or proven incapacity at that time. Besides, several preventive measures can help reduce the risk of developing the cancer, such as enhanced surveillance (e.g. regular mammography or MRI), surgery (e.g. removal of both breasts) and lifestyle adjustments.
>
> [Carys’ story](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cl40ex5rrnko.amp) was widely covered in the national media. Following public scrutiny, the British Army reviewed and reversed its decision, confirming Carys was medically fit to join. According to the Army, no policy of discrimination was involved, but a process mistake had resulted in an erroneous rejection.

As science and society evolve, it is difficult to predict how genomic information will be used in the future. Harm does not arise only from proven cases of discrimination; the [anticipation of stigma or exclusion](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-471X/7/2/17) can also negatively affect people’s psychological wellbeing and social relationships. It may also [discourage individuals from doing genetic testing](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cl40ex5rrnko.amp) or participating in research, potentially leading to missed diagnoses and lost opportunities for prevention or early treatment.

> **Real story – Insurance protection in Australia**
>
> For a long time, Australian life insurance companies could ask for and use people’s genetic test results to decide whether they would offer coverage or increase premiums. Because people feared that their genetic results could make it harder or more expensive to get life insurance, many avoided genetic testing in clinical and research settings, despite proven health benefits.
>
> In September 2024, the Australian government announced a [legislated ban](https://bridges.monash.edu/articles/report/_strong_Final_Stakeholder_Report_of_the_strong_em_strong_Australian_Genetics_and_Life_Insurance_Moratorium_Monitoring_the_Effectiveness_and_Response_A-GLIMMER_strong_em_strong_Project_strong_/23564538/1?file=41361345). Since then, life insurers are no longer allowed to use genetic test results that show a health risk when underwriting insurance. Yet, people are still free to [disclose results in their favour](https://www.monash.edu/news/articles/australian-government-bans-genetic-discrimination-in-life-insurance-a-big-win-for-preventive-health), for instance, to show that they are not carriers of a genetic variant known within their family.
>
> This revision of the law is significant for several reasons. First, people can now undergo genetic testing with greater peace of mind, as they cannot be penalised by health insurers based on their genetic results. From a clinical perspective, it means more people may decide to take a genetic test that can identify health risks early, making it possible to start treatment or take preventive steps before serious illness develops. From a research perspective, it encourages people to participate in scientific projects on genetics, which can lead to important new knowledge and advances in medical care. Second, a legal protection against discrimination helps build public trust. When people are reassured that their data can’t be used against them, they’re more willing to take part in genomic health initiatives in general, which are critical for advancing genomic medicine.

## Protection and empowerment

Vulnerability arising from the misuse of genomic data can be reduced through stringent and comprehensive data protection measures, laws and enforcement mechanisms. At the EU level, these include frameworks such as the [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng), [anti-discrimination legislation](https://fra.europa.eu/en/eu-charter/article/21-non-discrimination), the [AI Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) and the [European Health Data Space](https://health.ec.europa.eu/ehealth-digital-health-and-care/european-health-data-space-regulation-ehds_en). These instruments play an essential role in protecting privacy and preventing unauthorised access or discriminatory practices. Still, no legal system can prevent all misuse. Some discrimination may occur informally or unintentionally—for example, in workplace cultures, social interactions or family settings—where legal protections are harder to enforce.

For this reason, legal safeguards must be complemented by robust and transparent governance frameworks that promote responsible and trustworthy use of genomic data. Public engagement can positively contribute in this regard. When citizens are involved as partners in shaping genomic policies and practices, it enables them to integrate public values as a central element, thereby transforming some vulnerabilities into empowerment. Embedding shared values, such as solidarity, accountability, transparency and collective benefit into governance frameworks helps ensure that progress in genomics serves society first, rather than commercial or technical interests.
