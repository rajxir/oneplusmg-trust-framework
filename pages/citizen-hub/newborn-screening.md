## Newborn screening

Newborn screening (NBS)—also known as newborn blood spot screening or neonatal testing—helps detect serious but treatable [congenital conditions](glossary#congenital-disease) in babies, at birth. By taking a few drops of blood soon after delivery, it can identify, for instance, cystic fibrosis (or mucoviscidosis), spinal muscular atrophy, blood disorders and hearing loss. Those conditions may not show symptoms immediately, but they can cause severe health problems if untreated. Early detection and diagnosis (before symptoms appear) allow for timely treatment, monitoring and preventive care. This improves the quality of life and ensures every child has equal access to lifesaving care.

Aside from those benefits, NBS also raises important ethical and societal points of discussion, including the range of diseases that should be tested and the reuse of data generated by the test.

## How broad should newborn screening be?

NBS is now offered in all European countries and the UK, but the specific approach varies from country to country. For instance, the number of [diseases screened](https://media.crai.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/28135510/CRA-Insights-NBS-Policy-Updated-28-February-2024-vSTCCR.pdf) for varies considerably, from more than 30 conditions in countries like Italy, Portugal and Austria to as few as two in Cyprus. [This lack of harmonised EU criteria for disease inclusion](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://media.kbs-frb.be/fr/media/7601/20180704PP.pdf&sa=D&source=docs&ust=1795609705255825&usg=AOvVaw2rt3R3yG7eYUIaunBLvtUd) has sparked an ongoing debate about which diseases should be included in NBS programmes ([see an overview of conditions currently included across Europe](https://media.crai.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/28135510/CRA-Insights-NBS-Policy-Updated-28-February-2024-vSTCCR.pdf)).

Patient associations have argued that the lack of standardisation creates inequalities ([see EURORDIS position on newborn screening](https://www.eurordis.org/our-priorities/diagnosis/newborn-screening/)). From the very start of life, children receive different levels of preventive, diagnostic and curative care across countries. To address this, patient groups are calling for common European criteria for disease inclusion as a step towards more equitable healthcare. If you would like to know more about the opinions and experiences of [rare disease](glossary#rare-disease) patients on NBS, [read the EURORDIS *Voices on Newborn Screening* report](https://www.eurordis.org/publications/voices-on-newborn-screening-rb-report/).

At the same time, countries have very different organisational, political and financial systems and therefore possess varying capacities for prevention, diagnosis and treatment. Because of these differences, a strict one-size-fits-all approach may be out of reach for Europe. Instead, some patient associations have suggested [11 broad principles to support a more harmonised EU approach to NBS](https://download2.eurordis.org/documents/pdf/eurordis_nbs_position_paper.pdf).

> **Real story – Unequal access to newborn screening in Spain**
>
> For years, NBS in Spain varied widely between regions. Some autonomous communities tested only a handful of diseases, while others screened for more than 30. This meant a baby’s access to early diagnosis and treatment depended on where they were born. Families in regions with fewer tests described the situation as a ‘[postcode lottery](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34294672/&sa=D&source=docs&ust=1795609705256358&usg=AOvVaw0AgmSawWZRZ2Pqbuve7oRa)’ for their children’s health.
>
> For a detailed overview of newborn screening practices in Spain, [see this analysis of national and regional programmes](https://www.oaepublish.com/articles/rdodj.2023.14). Health professionals have argued that broader screening would protect children and reduce long-term costs (for more information, [read the perspectives of clinicians on expanding screening](https://www.analesdepediatria.org/en-newborn-screening-for-metabolic-disorders-articulo-S2341287919301164)).

Hence, even if common standards were agreed across Europe, the pressing question remains: if feasible, should all babies be routinely screened for the widest possible range of diseases to give every child the best chance of a healthy start in life?

Currently, NBS only includes conditions where early detection clearly improves health through prevention, monitoring or timely treatment. However, with rapid advances in [genomics](glossary#genomics), there is growing debate about expanding the scope, or even [sequencing](glossary#sequencing) the entire [genome](glossary#genome) of every newborn ([see the UK policy discussion on whole genome sequencing for newborn screening](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/999931/WGS_for_newborn_screening_FINAL_ACCESSIBLE.pdf)).

Autonomy is a first challenge. Parents may wish to know everything about their child’s genetic makeup, but as children grow, they also have a right to make their own health decisions.

A second challenge is that [whole genome sequencing](glossary#wgs) could shift how society views health. If risks are systematically identified at birth, almost everyone may be seen as a ‘pre-patient’—healthy for now, but predisposed to future diseases.

This last point raises another challenge: the psychological impact on families. Learning about a potential health risk in an otherwise healthy baby can cause distress and uncertainty ([see the EURORDIS report on lived experiences and impacts of newborn screening](https://www.eurordis.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/RB_NBS_report_vff.pdf)).

> **Real story: The Pompe disease**
>
> In Italy, doctors expanded newborn screening to include [Pompe disease](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://pompediseasenews.com/news/newborn-screening-pompe-italy-reports-successful-outcomes/&sa=D&source=docs&ust=1795609705256724&usg=AOvVaw0e-xdnMLHIdEf2zwDL0ykS), a rare condition that can be fatal in babies if untreated. Between 2015 and 2022, more than 200,000 newborns were tested. Three were diagnosed with the severe infant form of Pompe disease and, thanks to immediate treatment, had a far better chance of healthy development.
>
> Yet, the programme also detected genetic abnormalities in babies who were unlikely ever to become ill. Some babies were [carriers](glossary) with no actual health risks; others had harmless variants that triggered abnormal test results. In some cases, doctors were not sure if a gene change would ever lead to illness. For families, these results created stress and uncertainty, sometimes leading to years of follow-up visits without clear answers.
>
> This case illustrates both sides of expanded NBS: it can save lives by detecting rare but severe disease early, but it can also cause anxiety and burdensome follow-up for children.

## Potential uses of newborn screening data

Health data collected at birth can be valuable beyond its original purpose, for instance, to support public health statistics, research and policymaking. With initiatives such as the [European Health Data Space](https://health.ec.europa.eu/ehealth-digital-health-and-care/european-health-data-space-regulation-ehds_en) (EHDS), the reuse of NBS data is expected to grow within health-related contexts. Yet, data could also be reused outside of health, for instance, in forensic investigations, which are not covered by the EHDS.

---

> **Real story: NBS data reused to solve a crime in Sweden**
>
> In 2003, [Sweden's Foreign Minister Anna Lindh](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://swedishonlinearticle.blogspot.com/2016/06/pku-registry-opens-to-police-aftonbladet.html&sa=D&source=docs&ust=1795609705259361&usg=AOvVaw2hLsI5vSQUqZiYmasrwTg5) was murdered in a department store. Police investigators found no match between the DNA left behind by the killer and the national criminal database, so they turned to the PKU biobank — a collection of blood samples from all newborns in Sweden since 1975, originally assembled as a medical screening resource. The police identified and arrested a suspect using DNA from that biobank.
>
> The [case](https://www.google.com/url?q=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4805715/&sa=D&source=docs&ust=1795609705258966&usg=AOvVaw3s_Vz-veifNLpw7X0T7tYZ) became widely publicised and sparked a major public debate. Approximately 2,000 people subsequently requested the destruction of their previously stored PKU samples after the forensic use of the biobank was covered by the media.
>
> Following this case, a practice emerged requiring a court order for police to access the PKU registry. However, the deputy biobank officer at the time described the situation as a "trust issue" and warned that the registry could become less complete, and consequently less useful for its intended purpose, if police access were normalised.

This case raises the question of how transparency, consent and trust can be safeguarded while at the same time exploring the new opportunities offered by genomic technologies.

Finally, when considering the reuse of NBS data, autonomy is key. Parents give consent at birth, but as children grow, they may wish to decide for themselves how their genetic data is reused. This raises questions such as: How long should parents decide on their child’s behalf? At what age should children be asked for consent? How can future autonomy be protected while ensuring society still benefits from research?

These debates highlight a central tension in balancing individual rights with the broader societal value of research. Decisions made at the very start of life can carry consequences for decades to come—for both individuals and society.
